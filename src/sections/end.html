The bookshelf

You asked how to learn this properly rather than from a pile of mediocre tutorials. The honest answer is to use few sources, all of them primary: the official books, the standard library's docs, docs written by a crate's own author, and a handful of books by people who build these systems. Skip "Rust in an hour" videos, posts written before the 2018 edition, and any tutorial you can't trace to someone who ships Rust. Here is the shelf, in roughly the order it becomes useful.

Rust

The Rust Programming Language, Klabnik, Nichols and Krycho (free online)
The official book. Each ticket's "Go deeper" points at the chapters it just made you need. Read them then, not before.
The standard library docs
Better written than most books. When you wonder whether something exists, it's usually a method on Option, Iterator or str.
Rust by Example (free online)
Small runnable programs, one feature at a time. Good for "show me the syntax" moments.
Programming Rust, 2nd edition, Blandy, Orendorff and Tindall (O'Reilly, 2021)
The best second book. Deeper than the official one on ownership, traits, iterators and collections, written by systems programmers for systems programmers. Read it during part 1 and 2.
Rust for Rustaceans, Jon Gjengset (No Starch, 2021)
How experienced Rust developers think: API design, testing, error handling at scale, what unsafe is for. Read it after part 2, when your code has enough shape to be worth arguing about.
Zero to Production in Rust, Luca Palmieri (self-published)
Building and testing a real web service. It uses actix-web rather than the axum you'll use in part 3, but its habits (tests first, configuration, structured logging, error types) are what matter. Part 3's companion.
Rust Atomics and Locks, Mara Bos (O'Reilly, 2023, free online)
Concurrency from the hardware up, by a member of the Rust library team. For parts 2 and 3.
The Rust API Guidelines and the Clippy lint list
What idiomatic means, written down. Read a few clippy lint explanations a week: each one is a small lesson about why.

Software design

A Philosophy of Software Design, John Ousterhout (Yaknyam Press, 2nd edition 2021)
Short and opinionated, about the thing your reviews keep circling: how to split a program into pieces that hide their complexity. If you read one book on design, read this one, after ticket 4.
Designing Data-Intensive Applications, Martin Kleppmann (O'Reilly)
How real data systems handle failure, duplicates, ordering and time. Part 2's tickets are small versions of its chapters.

Computer science

Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective, 3rd edition, Bryant and O'Hallaron (Pearson, 2015)
Memory, caches, linking, processes, networking: everything underneath your program, explained for the person writing it. Ticket 7 leans on chapter 6.
Introduction to Algorithms, 4th edition, Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein (MIT Press, 2022)
The reference. Dip in, don't read it cover to cover. The Algorithm Design Manual by Steven Skiena is the friendlier companion.
"What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic", David Goldberg (1991)
The paper behind ticket 3. Heavy going in places, and with your math background, the most rewarding thing on this list.
Numerical Linear Algebra, Trefethen and Bau (SIAM, 1997)
For part 4: why the textbook formula for least squares is the wrong one to compute with, and what to do instead.
